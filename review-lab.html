Lab 09 · the highest-value AI output

Review

review.md calls the weekly review "the highest-value AI output in the app — it is the moment the app becomes something other than a tracker." It is also the app's most constrained artefact: two paragraphs, no prescription, no ranking, no gamification, and it must cite its sources and their freshness.

The module is well specified — the nudge, the queue merge and the tone rules all check out against the attention budget, down to the exact urgency and TTL. The defects are inside the document. Its own example breaks three of its rules, and one of them has already propagated into a second doc.

The review is a note

review.md §The review card gives the review three presentations — a nudge, a note, a calendar card — and surfaces.md agrees, listing three rows. The middle one is the one that matters, because it decides what reading the app's highest-value output actually is:

"Once opened, the review is a Note attached to the Day. It lives in the Notes mode and is searchable."

notes.md lists exactly four note surfaces: List, Editor, Note detail (read-only) and Search. So the surface the review opens on is notes.md's Editor — "A markdown editor. Title field at top, body below. Autosaves on blur and every 5 seconds while typing. No save button."

Against rules
One: 05-modules/protocols.md establishes the opposite for the other generated note — "The user may edit a report; the edit is a note.edited entry… There is no separate 'original preserved' UI." Two generated notes, two rules.
Frontend design

separate the container from the presentation

The note stays the storage form — attached to the Day, searchable, in the Notes mode. What it gains is a reader: the two paragraphs at type-body width, the sources in a footer, and Edit behind the overflow.

The review is the app's only output that is written to be read once and well, and its only reading surface is built for writing.

Changed
Adds a reading surface for generated notes, leaving storage unchanged. The note keeps its attachment, its search presence and its editability; it stops opening on an editor by default.
Cost
A fourth note surface for one kind of note — notes.md lists four and this makes five, for a note the user did not write. And the review is meant to be edited: it is a note because the doc wanted it to live with the user's own writing.
Against rules
One: surfaces.md lists the review note as a Screen distinct from notes.md's Editor, so the index already assumes a separate surface that no doc specifies. This makes the index true.
Design taste

Don't put the review in Recent. It is output, not writing.

cut the tell · a list of your own notes
Recent your notes only

Reviews move to their own list — the existing Daily scope already holds day-attached notes, and notes.md already says notes attached by other flows appear under Recent and All. This makes generated notes a scope rather than prose the user's own notes share a list with.

Cost: the review stops being discoverable beside the writing it is about, and a user who never opens the new scope never finds their review again.

Changed
Generated notes leave Recent, which is defined as "Notes edited in the last 14 days" — a definition the review satisfies while belonging to the app rather than the user.
Cost
Recent is the Notes mode's default scope. Moving reviews out of it means the app's highest-value output is on a secondary list, and the doc currently argues the opposite: the review lives in the Notes mode precisely so it sits beside the user's writing.
Against rules
Two: §The review card says the review "lives in the Notes mode and is searchable", and notes.md's Daily scope explicitly anticipates day-attached notes by other flows appearing under Recent and All. Both docs put it there deliberately.
Recommended

Keep it a note, and give the Sources block a rule.

one generated-note rule for both generated notes

The fix is one rule in notes.md, applied to both generated notes (the review and the protocol report), and one line in each:

"A generated note's Sources block is part of the artefact, not the body. It is editable, and note.edited records the change — but the block is rendered from the event's own metadata, so an edit to it is a correction the user can see, not a silent one."

Changed
Nothing about the surfaces. One shared rule for generated notes, and a Sources block that says what it is — a record taken at generation — instead of asserting a freshness that decays. Invariant 4 is satisfied and stays satisfied.
Cost
It concedes that a generated note's citation can be edited, which means the invariant is enforced by convention rather than by the model. And a block "rendered from the event's own metadata" is a new rendering path for a note body part — the app's only non-markdown region inside one.
Still needs
Whether the review is the user's note or the app's. Every surface decision above follows from that one, and the doc leans both ways: the review lives with the user's writing (so it is theirs) and it cites its sources (so it is a record). The lab can show the consequence of each — there is no reading that satisfies both, because a note the user owns is a note the user can change.

Changed

Cost

Against rules
One, and it has already spread: "Sep 16–22" appears three times in review.md and once in 06-flows/retrieval.md's example. A wrong example propagates as a copy, which is the case for deriving the range rather than writing it.
Design taste

The prescription is the example being honest. Move the rule.

cut the tell · the boundary is in the wrong place

Disallowed · "prescribes"

“You should walk before meditating.”
“Put the walk on the calendar.”

Allowed · what the example does

“Your meditation habit correlated with days you walked first (6 of 7). A consistent order would make the correlation testable.”

The line to hold is between changing the user's day and describing what the data suggests. "Consider putting the walk before meditation" crosses it; naming the pattern and its strength does not.

Changed
The rule is made precise: the review may describe a correlation and may not direct a change. The example's sentence is rewritten to stay on the describing side, which is what the rest of the paragraph already does.
Cost
"A consistent order would make the correlation testable" is the weak version of the same thought, and the strong version was the useful one. The doc's P2 explicitly allows a "forward-looking observation", which is nearly a suggestion — so the line may be in the wrong place rather than the example being wrong.
Against rules
None — and note the direction: the example is doing the thing the doc wanted and the rule is stated too broadly. That is the same shape as the Week view's finding, where the spec's only sentence was the thing every variant had to break.
Recommended

Derive the numbers, derive the range, and narrow the rule.

Changed
Three fixes to the doc, none to the module. (1) The Sources line is derived from the same query as the prose, so its numbers cannot disagree. (2) The window is the seven days ending at the trigger — Sep 14–20 — and the four places carrying "Sep 16–22" are corrected, including retrieval.md's. (3) The no-prescription rule is narrowed to forbid directing a change to the user's day while allowing the correlation to be named as the example does.
Cost
Deriving the Sources line means four aggregates must be available to the renderer independently of the prose, so the review's generation is split into a query and a composition where the doc currently describes one Tier 3 pass. That is a real change to how the app produces its highest-value output.
Still needs
What the Sources line is for. Invariant 4 says the review cites its sources and their freshness, which is a claim about trust — but the numbers are the review's own front page restated, and the freshness decays (see the next screen). If the citation's purpose is auditability, it wants the query and the moment; if it is reassurance, it wants neither. The doc never says which, and the block's shape follows.

Freshness

Invariant 4: "The review cites its data sources and their freshness." The block that satisfies it reads:

Calendar as of 2 hours ago. Health as of 8 hours ago.

That is a relative claim written into a static document. The same section says the note "remains in the Notes mode; the user can read it at any time" — so the document whose invariant is that it states freshness contains a freshness statement that stops being true the moment it is stored.

Baseline

The same note, read at four points after generation.

no lens · the control arm
Read immediately “Calendar as of 2 hours ago”true
Read 24 hours later still says 2 hoursoff by 24h
Read a week later still says 2 hoursoff by 7d
Read a month later still says 2 hoursoff by 30d

The error is not approximate — it is exactly the time since generation. The statement's accuracy decays at one second per second, which is the one property a freshness claim must not have.

Changed
Nothing. The freshness line as specified, read at four intervals.
Cost
…
Against rules
Two, and they pull the same way. Invariant 4 here requires the freshness to be cited; the app's freshness invariant elsewhere (02-architecture/data-lifecycle.md) requires that the user never be shown a value that silently changed. A relative "as of" in a stored document violates the second while trying to satisfy the first.
Impeccable

Record the absolute moment; let the reading surface compute the age.

store the fact, render the relative

Stored: calendar snapshot 2026-09-20T17:58+01:00 · health snapshot 2026-09-20T11:00+01:00

Read immediately renders “2 hours ago”true
Read a week later renders “7 days ago”true
Read a month later renders “30 days ago”true

Two timestamps in the note's metadata, one rendered phrase, and it is never wrong — which is what "as of" was trying to say.

Changed
The freshness becomes two absolute timestamps recorded by the generator, and the reading surface renders the relative phrase from them at the moment of reading.
Cost
The phrase is now part of the rendering rather than the note, so it is not searchable, not copied when the note is shared, and not visible in a plain-text export. And the note's body stops being self-contained markdown — the doc calls notes "markdown" with no exceptions.
Against rules
One: notes.md says notes are markdown and lists what is supported, with no provision for a computed region. This is the same shape as the Sources-block question on the previous screen, and both want the same answer.
Frontend design

Keep the relative phrase and date the note: "2 hours before this review".

anchor to the artefact, not to now

Calendar was 2 hours old when this review was generated. Health was 8 hours old.

Read immediately same sentencetrue
Read a week later same sentencetrue
Read a year later same sentencetrue

The sentence is still markdown, still searchable, still exportable — and it is a statement about generation, which is a fact that cannot change. The reader who wants "how old is this now" has the note's own date.

Changed
One word: the phrase moves from the present tense to the past. "As of 2 hours ago" asserts a relation to now; "was 2 hours old when this review was generated" asserts a relation to the review, which is fixed.
Cost
It answers a different question. The user reading a month-old review may well want to know how stale the underlying data is right now — and this sentence deliberately refuses to say, leaving them to subtract. It also makes the note's own date load-bearing, and the note's date is the review's scheduled date, not its generation time, which the doc explicitly separates.
Against rules
One, and it is subtle: §Invariants says a late review is "attached to the review's scheduled date, not the open date". So the note's date is the Sunday, while the freshness timestamps are the Wednesday — and a sentence anchored to generation would sit under a title anchored to the schedule, four days apart.
Design taste

Cut the freshness line. The review is about the week, and the week is past.

cut the tell · a stale caveat is worse than none

Sources: 14 tasks, 4 habits, 6 events, 2 protocols.

A review of Sep 14–20 is a statement about a window that has closed. Nothing in it can go stale, because nothing in it claims to be current — so the freshness clause has no referent and the citation keeps only the counts.

But Invariant 4 names freshness explicitly, and 02-architecture/data-lifecycle.md requires the annotation wherever a mirrored value is shown. Deleting it makes the doc's own invariant false.

Changed
Deletes the freshness clause. The Sources line keeps the aggregates and drops the portion that cannot stay true.
Cost
The review's own example cites "Calendar as of 2 hours ago", which means the Calendar was mirrored — and mirrored data is the app's designated case for a freshness annotation. Hiding it means a review can present a synced event as fact with no indication of when the sync last ran.
Against rules
Two: Invariant 4 in this doc, and the freshness requirement in data-lifecycle.md. This is the one row in the lab that trades a rule for tidiness, and the rule is not this module's to trade.
Recommended

Say what was true at generation, and give the note both dates.

anchor to the artefact · keep the invariant
Weekly review — Sep 14–20

Sources: 14 tasks, 24/28 checks, 6 events, 2 protocols.
Snapshots taken 20 Sep 18:00 — calendar 2h old, health 8h old.

Both dates are present and they are different facts: the window (Sep 14–20) and the snapshot (20 Sep 18:00). The freshness clause is anchored to the snapshot, so it is permanently true, and the note's scheduled-date attachment is untouched.

Changed
The freshness clause gains an anchor — the snapshot moment — so it describes the generation rather than the reading. This also separates two things §Invariants currently conflates by giving the note one date: which week this is about and when it was produced. The late-generation case needs both, and the doc gives one.
Cost
Two dates on one note is a small piece of bookkeeping the user now has to read, and in the ordinary case they are minutes apart, so the second date is noise on 51 reviews a year. It is load-bearing only on the review generated late — which §Invariants handles by making the note's date not the generation time.
Still needs
Which date is the review's identity. §Invariants attaches a late review to its scheduled date "not the open date" — so the app already decided the scheduled date wins, and a review generated the following Wednesday is filed under a Sunday. That is the right call for grouping and the wrong one for freshness, and the doc argues for one date while needing two.

Monthly and annual

review.md §The annual and monthly reviews: "Monthly review. 4 weeks aggregated. Generated on the first of the month. Annual review. 12 months aggregated. Generated on January 1."

The monthly review's window is a duration and its trigger is a calendar boundary, and the two do not agree for eleven months of the year. The annual review then multiplies the disagreement: twelve of the monthly windows cover 336 days.

Baseline

4 weeks, on the first of the month, twelve times.

no lens · the control arm

Each row is a month the trigger fires and the window the rule gives it. The uncovered days are never reviewed by the monthly surface.

Changed
Nothing. "4 weeks aggregated", fired on the first of each month.
Cost
…
Against rules
One: the window and the trigger are two different definitions of a month, and the doc states both in one sentence without noticing. The annual review then inherits the question — its "12 months" is a calendar year, so it does not aggregate twelve monthly reviews.
Impeccable

The window is the calendar month the trigger closes.

derive the range from the trigger · one month, one meaning

"The previous calendar month, 1st to last." Twelve of them tile the year exactly, and the annual review aggregates the same twelve windows it already has.

Changed
The window becomes "the previous calendar month" rather than 4 weeks. The monthly and annual reviews become nested rather than two independent windows, and no day is uncovered.
Cost
Calendar months are unequal — 28 to 31 days — so the monthly review's numbers are not comparable between months, which is the one thing a monthly aggregate is for. "You completed 62 tasks" means something different in February than in March, and the review has no way to say so without printing the window length.
Against rules
One: §The annual and monthly reviews fixes "4 weeks aggregated", and 4 weeks is at least a duration — comparable by construction. The row trades comparability for coverage, and the doc chose the other way.
Frontend design

Keep 4 weeks and state the gaps: a rolling 13-week quarter.

13 × 4 = 52 weeks, so nothing is lost
Monthly 4 weeks, on the 1st12 × 28 = 336d
Annual 12 months aggregated365d
Never covered by the monthly surface29 days
13 four-week windows 52 weeks364d

An annual review generated on January 1 covers 12 months. A monthly review generated on the 1st covers 4 weeks. Over a year the monthly windows leave 29 days unaccounted for — and the annual review's own "12 months" is a third window that matches neither.

Changed
Nothing changes in the product. The doc states the relationship: the monthly window is 4 weeks, twelve of them are 336 days, and the annual review's window is a calendar year — so the two longer reviews are independent windows, not nested ones. That is a legitimate choice and it has to be written down, because otherwise 29 days a year vanish silently.
Cost
Writing it down makes the gap permanent rather than fixing it, and a user who reads that their monthly reviews cover 336 days a year will reasonably ask what happened to the rest. The fix that removes the question is a window derived from the trigger, which this row rejects.
Against rules
None — and it is the least invasive row here, because the annual review being a separate window is arguably what an annual review is. What it cannot do is make the three windows line up, and the doc's three sentences currently read as if they do.
Design taste

Delete the annual review. A year is 52 weekly reviews, and they are already there.

cut the tell · an aggregate of aggregates
Weekly the primary output52/yr
Monthly optional, off by default12/yr
Annual what the weekly reviews already contain removed

A year's narrative is 52 two-paragraph reviews plus a search. Adding a 53rd that summarises them is the app generating a document about its own documents.

But the annual review is the only surface that looks at a year, and "the moment the app becomes something other than a tracker" is a claim about perspective — which is exactly what a year gives and a week cannot.

Changed
Deletes the annual review, leaving weekly and monthly. The year is reachable by reading the weekly reviews, which the doc already says remain readable forever.
Cost
The annual review is where a year-long pattern could be named — a habit that changed in March and held, a protocol's effect eight months on. Nothing else in the app has a window that long except the Year view, which shows busyness rather than meaning. Deleting it removes the only surface that could say "this year, this changed".
Against rules
Two: §The annual and monthly reviews specifies it, and surfaces.md's "Review note (weekly / monthly / annual)" row names all three in one entry — so deleting one means editing a row that treats them as a set.
Recommended

One window rule: the period the trigger closes, for all three.

fix the definition once
Weekly 7 days ending at the triggerSep 14–20
Monthly the calendar month the trigger closes Sep 1–30
Annual the calendar year the trigger closes 2026
Nesting 52 weeks = 12 months = 1 year exact

One sentence replaces three: a review covers the period its trigger closes. The weekly keeps its seven days, the monthly becomes a month, and the annual becomes a year — and the three nest, so the annual review can be computed from the monthly windows the app already produced.

Changed
One definition governs all three windows, so the nesting is exact and no day is unreviewed. The weekly is unchanged (its trigger is already periodic), and this is the same fix the previous screen's date-range row needs — one rule for "which days is this review about".
Cost
It gives up the one property "4 weeks" had: equal-length windows. Monthly totals stop being comparable month to month, which for a report whose whole job is comparison is a real loss — and the doc may have chosen 4 weeks for exactly that reason without saying so.
Still needs
Whether comparability or coverage matters more for the monthly review. A 4-week window makes "you completed 62 tasks" mean the same thing every month; a calendar month makes it true for the month and different every time. The lab can show that the doc's current answer is neither — it states a 4-week window and fires it on a calendar boundary — so the decision is open rather than made, and the doc should make it.
